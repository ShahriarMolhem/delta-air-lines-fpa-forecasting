# Delta Air Lines — Slide-by-Slide Presentation Practice Script

**Presenter:** Shahriar Molhem · University of Georgia, Terry College of Business  
**Time:** About 8–10 minutes, depending on pace. Use natural American professional English. Rehearse rather than memorize word-for-word.

## Slide 1 — Introduction
Hi, I'm Shahriar Molhem, a finance student at the University of Georgia. This project examines Delta Air Lines using publicly available financial statements and operating statistics. My goal was to learn how financial and operating information can support budgeting and forecasting decisions. This is an independent undergraduate project, not an internal Delta budget.

## Slide 2 — Questions
I built the analysis around three questions. How did revenue and operating income change? Which cost categories explain the arithmetic change between 2024 and 2025? And how would hypothetical changes in capacity, passenger yield and fuel prices affect an operating plan? I kept historical reported facts separate from forecast assumptions.

## Slide 3 — Historical context
Delta's reported revenue rose substantially between 2021 and 2025, from approximately 29.9 billion dollars to 63.4 billion dollars. But the pandemic makes 2021 an unusual comparison year. Delta also recognized government payroll grant support in that period, so I focus more on the latest two years when talking about recent margin pressure.

## Slide 4 — Operating margins
Delta's reported operating revenue increased about 2.8 percent from 2024 to 2025, while operating income declined by 173 million dollars. That moved the operating margin from about 9.7 percent to 9.2 percent. As an FP&A analyst, I would want to look beyond top-line growth and consider whether cost increases are offsetting revenue gains.

## Slide 5 — Reconciliation bridge
I reconciled 2024 operating income of 5 billion 995 million dollars to 2025's 5 billion 822 million. Passenger revenue grew 874 million, cargo grew 78 million and other revenue grew 769 million. Higher wages reduced profit by 1 billion 359 million; lower fuel costs added 747 million, and other operating expense changes reduced profit by another 1 billion 282 million. This is an accounting bridge, not a claim of one specific underlying cause.

## Slide 6 — Fuel drivers
Fuel expense fell by 747 million dollars even though reported gallons consumed increased from 4.114 to 4.269 billion gallons. I used reported fuel expense divided by gallons to calculate an implied unit expense, then separated the expense change into price and consumption components. This demonstrates how a lower unit cost can offset higher consumption, although it does not explain all hedging or refinery effects.

## Slide 7 — Planning assumptions
Next I constructed a hypothetical operating model for 2026 to 2028. It forecasts passenger revenue using available seat miles and passenger revenue per seat mile. Fuel costs depend on modeled gallons and unit expense; labor depends on capacity and unit labor cost. These future inputs are independent student assumptions—not company guidance.

## Slide 8 — Scenario results
In my illustrative 2026 base case, revenue reaches about 65.6 billion dollars, but operating income falls to approximately 5.26 billion dollars, with an operating margin around 8 percent. Although revenue grows, the modeled labor and other unit costs increase faster than passenger revenue yield. This is a cost-pressure stress case, not a prediction about Delta's actual 2026 earnings.

## Slide 9 — Sensitivity
I tested the effects of different modeled passenger yields and fuel prices. The center of the sensitivity table returns about 5.257 billion dollars in operating income. A 10 percent rise in the modeled fuel unit expense lowers this by about 1 billion dollars, holding everything else unchanged. The lesson is that scenario results can change significantly when important drivers move, and that real-world demand responses are more complex than a one-variable test.

## Slide 10 — Conclusion
This project helped me connect SEC reporting, airline operating metrics, Excel forecasting and communication. My key takeaway is that strong revenue growth does not automatically guarantee profit growth, especially in a cost-sensitive industry. I would improve the model with more detailed segment costs and updated operating information in a future version. Thank you for listening—I'm happy to explain the workbook or its assumptions.

## Practice strategy
1. Read the full script with each slide visible and verify the fiscal year, unit and direction of every number.
2. Record a run-through, then redo it using only the slide titles and three key points per slide.
3. Answer without notes: **Why is 2021 unusual? Why did fuel expense fall as gallons rose? Why are 2026 model numbers not an actual Delta budget?**

**Language tip:** Say “F-P-and-A”, “A-S-M”, “P-RASM”, and “E-bit”. Use “percentage points” for changes between two percentages. Do not imply audited analysis or independently established expertise.


## Delivery notes: keep the financial story conversational

A natural presentation explains the business change first, then uses the financial statements to support the explanation. Avoid reading every number in the sensitivity table.

- **Slide 3 → 4:** “Because 2021 is a recovery year, I paid more attention to the changes between 2024 and 2025.”
- **Slide 5 → 6:** “Fuel is a good example of why unit costs and consumption should be separated.”
- **Slide 7 → 8:** “These aren't Delta's forecasts. I selected assumptions to explore what would happen if costs grew faster than yield.”
- **Slide 9:** Describe the *center* case first, then one higher-fuel-cost case. That's enough to convey the lesson.

**Practice interview questions:** What is ASM? Why should non-passenger revenue not be treated as air fares? What might break the assumption that all remaining expenses scale with ASM?

**If challenged:** “You're right that the model simplifies that relationship. I wanted a clear driver-based starting point; I'd refine the cost categories using segment data and the company's disclosures.”
